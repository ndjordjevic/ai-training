# Build a dim factory around sdlc-ticket

Almost nobody who ships real software runs a fully "dark" factory. The few who do (StrongDM's three-person team) did not remove human judgment. They moved it into specs and hidden test scenarios. Everyone else builds a "dim" factory: agents do the middle of the flow on their own, and humans keep three things. They decide what gets built, they accept what landed, and they approve anything that cannot be undone. Your SDLC is already most of a dim factory. `sdlc-ticket` chains intake through to verify. Its start round works as a policy, its end report works as a human queue, and `status.sh` and `board.sh` already read state from files. The missing parts are a trigger, a run record, a decision inbox and machine credentials. The best intake for one engineer without Jira admin rights is **polling Jira with JQL every 2–5 minutes**. Push options (an Automation rule, webhooks, Copilot for Jira) all need admin rights, and push should only come in later with the poll kept as a backup. The dashboard should be a small local app on SQLite with the "needs you" queue at the top. The hard constraint is the runtime. At work the agent has to be Copilot CLI, so the factory core must be runtime-neutral, and you must prove that your skills run under `copilot -p` before anything else.

## Dark factories exist, but practitioners keep humans at the edges

"Dark factory" is the top rung (Level 5) of Dan Shapiro's five-level ladder: specs go in, software comes out, and no human writes or reviews the code. Level 4 is the realistic step below it. You write specs and skills, review plans, then "leave for 12 hours, and check to see if the tests pass" ([Dan Shapiro](https://www.danshapiro.com/blog/2026/01/the-five-levels-from-spicy-autocomplete-to-the-software-factory/)). StrongDM is the main real Level 5 example. Its rules are that **code must not be written or reviewed by humans**. End-to-end "scenarios" are kept outside the codebase like a holdout set, and a probabilistic "satisfaction" score replaces a green test suite. They also built clones of Okta, Jira and Slack so agents can test without touching live systems ([Simon Willison](https://simonwillison.net/2026/Feb/7/software-factory/)). The holdout idea came from a real failure. Their agents wrote `return true`, which passed the tests and did nothing ([Stanford Law CodeX](https://law.stanford.edu/2026/02/08/built-by-agents-tested-by-agents-trusted-by-whom/)). StrongDM's own rule of thumb is **$1,000 of tokens per engineer per day**, which Willison puts at about $20k a month. Critics also point out that human work was "relocated upstream", not removed.

Your agentic AI wiki points the same way. Cole Medin's AI Software Factory, the most detailed production example in it, is "explicitly **not a dark factory**". Humans own the PRD and the judgment of what landed in staging. The factory also has three things worth copying: a holdout file that builders cannot read because a hook fences it off, protected paths the factory may never change, and a hard stop for a human on auth, identity and data-deletion work ([coleam00/ai-software-factory](https://github.com/coleam00/ai-software-factory); wiki: `wiki/sources/coleam00-ai-software-factory.md`). The ZazenCodes factory, built only from Claude Code skills and subagents, escalates anything that touches **money, auth, permissions, deploy/CI config, schema migrations, production data or new dependencies** ([zazencodes](https://github.com/zazencodes/zazencodes-season-3/tree/main/src/software-factory-claude-code); wiki: `wiki/sources/software-factory-claude-code.md`). MindStudio's explainer says "true production dark factories remain rare". Its example is Stripe's 1,000+ AI PRs a week, which still get human review ([MindStudio](https://www.mindstudio.ai/blog/what-is-dark-factory-autonomous-ai-codebase); wiki: `wiki/sources/dark-factory-autonomous-ai-codebase.md`).

Under the different branding, the designs share one structure. **OpenAI's Symphony SPEC** is the clearest open reference. A long-running service polls the tracker every 30 seconds and claims eligible issues. It runs one workspace per issue, caps concurrency, and retries with backoff of `min(10s × 2^(attempt−1), 5 min)`. A worker that stays silent for 5 minutes is killed. The tracker is the control plane: if a human moves the ticket out of an active state, the run stops ([Symphony SPEC](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/openai/symphony/main/SPEC.md); wiki: `wiki/sources/openai-symphony.md`). Inside each run, Anthropic's harness research supplies the rules: work on **one feature at a time**, keep state in files and git rather than in context, and use a separate, skeptical evaluator. In Anthropic's words, the agent alone "talked itself into deciding they weren't a big deal" ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/effective-harnesses-for-long-running-agents); [Anthropic harness design](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/harness-design-long-running-apps); wiki: `wiki/sources/anthropic.com-managed-agents.md`). Google's whitepaper sums up the stakes: "Agent = Model + Harness", and "most agent failures… are configuration failures" ([Whitepaper](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1IR7CddF_2FyQo_PdfBNTaEA50EGiVt2r/view); wiki: `wiki/sources/the-new-sdlc-with-vibe-coding.md`). For a regulated Australian fintech, the right target is **Level 4**: plan review and merge stay human, and Level 5 is a direction, not a policy.

## Your sdlc-ticket is already a factory run; four parts are missing

The 16 `sdlc-*` skills cover intake → plan → build → review → PR → summary → verify → QA → qa-fix. Every one has `disable-model-invocation: true`, so today nothing starts on its own ([our-sdlc](file:///Users/nenaddjordjevic/ai-training/our-sdlc.md); [sdlc-ticket](file:///Users/nenaddjordjevic/ai-training/.claude/skills/sdlc-ticket/SKILL.md)). Three design choices already match factory practice. First, **state lives in files**: intent, plan, checks and Build log markers such as "flow: build done", in the private worknotes repo. Your rule "if a fresh session can't continue from the files alone, the file is missing something" is the same idea as Anthropic's progress-file pattern. Second, **sdlc-ticket asks once at the start** (base branch, branch name, whether to commit and push, open a draft PR, add stubs or deploy to the lower env). After that it "investigates instead of asking" and writes down its assumptions. In a factory, that start round becomes a per-repo policy file. Third, **the end report is the human queue**: what blocks the merge, questions for the team, notes to send, and the next step ([flow-rules](file:///Users/nenaddjordjevic/ai-training/.claude/skills/sdlc-ticket/flow-rules.md)). `status.sh` and `board.sh` already give a deterministic read model per ticket and across tickets.

Four things are missing. There is **no trigger**: nothing reacts to "assigned to me". There is **no machine-readable run state**. The markers are grep-able text, but nothing records attempts, session ids, cost or "waiting on human". There are **no machine credentials**. AWS credentials are pasted into chat and expire mid-run, VPN and proxy switches are manual, and the mock console needs a human sign-in, so sdlc-ticket already treats credential expiry as "the only stop in the middle". Finally, **nothing comes after the PR**. There is no follow-through skill for review comments or CI, no release skill, and no Jira transitions. One more limit is deliberate and should stay: nothing is posted to Jira, GitHub or chat until you approve the exact text, and "the agent that wrote the code can never approve it" ([our-sdlc](file:///Users/nenaddjordjevic/ai-training/our-sdlc.md)). The factory must respect these rules, not route around them. In practice, drafted comments go into the decision inbox and never straight to Jira.

## Poll Jira with JQL first; push needs admin rights you don't have

There are five ways to get work from Jira. They differ mostly in who must set them up.

| Intake | Who can set it up | Latency | Fit for phase 1 |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Poll REST/JQL with a personal API token** | You alone | Poll interval (1–5 min) | **Best.** Outbound only, nothing exposed, survives restarts |
| Jira Automation "Send web request" → GitHub `workflow_dispatch` | Project admin | Seconds | Phase 2+. Uses the company's shared automation step allowance and sends ticket text to an external URL |
| Admin or dynamic webhooks | Site admin or a registered app | Seconds | Phase 3. Dynamic webhooks expire every 30 days |
| Copilot coding agent for Jira | Jira site admin + Rovo on + GitHub org owner | Seconds | Approved vendor, but runs GitHub's agent, not your skills and gates |
| Rovo Dev / Cursor / Agents in Jira | Admin + procurement | Seconds | Not phase 1 in a bank |

The table is based on these sources: ([Atlassian webhooks](https://developer.atlassian.com/cloud/jira/platform/webhooks/); [Atlassian automation usage](https://support.atlassian.com/cloud-automation/docs/how-is-my-usage-calculated/); [OpenAI Codex Jira cookbook](https://developers.openai.com/cookbook/examples/codex/jira-github); [GitHub Copilot for Jira](https://docs.github.com/en/copilot/how-tos/use-copilot-agents/coding-agent/integrate-coding-agent-with-jira)). Polling also stays cheap: **API-token traffic is outside the points-based rate limits** Atlassian brought in from March 2026 ([Atlassian rate limiting](https://developer.atlassian.com/cloud/jira/platform/rate-limiting)). Webhooks also behave worse than they look. Jira retries up to 5 times, **duplicates are possible**, and there is no ordering guarantee. That is why every serious orchestrator reconciles by re-reading the tracker anyway ([Atlassian webhooks](https://developer.atlassian.com/cloud/jira/platform/webhooks/)). Symphony itself uses plain polling with no database. A public tunnel from a corporate laptop to receive webhooks will probably break bank policy.

Use an opt-in trigger, not just "assigned to me". Every tool studied uses an explicit signal: an agent assignee, a label or a mention. One practitioner starts work only on an `auto` label, so a human always chooses what gets built ([Pierson Marks](https://www.piersonmarks.com/posts/building-a-software-factory)). A good starting JQL is `assignee = currentUser() AND status = "To Do" AND labels = agent-ready ORDER BY priority, created`. Treat every hit as a hint only. Re-read the ticket by key, take a lock per key, and re-check it before each step and before any push. If the ticket was unassigned, the label was removed or the status changed, stop and keep the branch. Removing the label or adding `agent-stop` cancels the run. Factory writes (its own labels such as `agent-running`) must never match the trigger, or the factory will retrigger itself. Labels and transitions need no admin rights, but custom fields do.

## The dashboard routes attention; decisions pause and resume the session

Existing tools all show the same core views: tickets by state, each in its own worktree, a live log, a diff or PR link, and a "needs input" state that stands out. Claude Code's agent view marks **Needs input in yellow** and is built around stepping in "only when a row needs you" ([agent view](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/agent-view#read-session-state)). Cole Medin's Factory Mission Control adds the operator views: an **owner queue**, steps running past 2× their usual duration, failed laps by cause, and **cost per merge** ([factory-mission-control](https://github.com/coleam00/factory-mission-control); wiki: `wiki/sources/coleam00-factory-mission-control.md`). The UX lesson is to "make state ambient", and to notify only when a decision is needed or a run has failed, never on success ([DEV: track multiple agents](https://dev.to/aidenwong/track-multiple-ai-agents-state-4bhj)).

Ready-made UIs fit badly. Vibe Kanban is closest, but its company shut down in April 2026, it runs agents with permission-skipping flags by default, and it keeps its own board instead of Jira ([VirtusLab](https://virtuslab.com/blog/ai/vibe-kanban/)). Build a small app instead: **FastAPI + SQLite (WAL mode) + server-rendered HTML with HTMX + SSE for live updates**, styled with Pico.css or Tailwind. It is one process with no JS build, and it copies Symphony's loopback dashboard with a JSON state API ([Symphony SPEC](https://github.com/openai/symphony/blob/main/SPEC.md)). Use four tables: `tickets` (stage mirrored from Jira), `runs` (one row per agent run, with session id, model, tokens, cost, log path), `decisions` and an append-only `events` table. Lay the page out with the attention queue on top, the board by stage in the middle and a live event feed below. Each run page links to the Jira ticket, the PR, the transcript and the `resume` command.

For human decisions, reuse the shape Claude already uses. `AskUserQuestion` sends a question, a header, 2–4 options with descriptions and optional free text ([Agent SDK user input](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/agent-sdk/user-input#handle-clarifying-questions)). Plain `claude -p` cannot ask anything unless the run has a permission host. Copilot CLI `-p` denies anything that needs approval, and `--no-ask-user` removes questions completely ([headless docs](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/headless#turn-off-permission-prompts-in-unattended-runs); [Copilot CLI programmatic reference](https://docs.github.com/en/copilot/reference/copilot-cli-reference/cli-programmatic-reference)). The simplest robust pattern is this: the run ends with a structured "needs decision" record, the factory saves the session id, and when you answer, it resumes that session with the answer ([sessions docs](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/agent-sdk/sessions#resume-by-id)). You don't need Temporal or Inngest until there are many machines or several reviewers. Every decision gets a deadline and a safe default: park the ticket, never approve. Record who answered, when and why.

## Operate it like production: pinned models, hard caps, sandbox, gates you don't let it edit

**Models.** Keep your existing split, because it rests on evidence: in the DSM-960 trial, Sonnet broke one or two intake rules on every run and Opus broke none ([our-sdlc](file:///Users/nenaddjordjevic/ai-training/our-sdlc.md)). That means Opus 5.5 for intake, plan and review, Sonnet 5.5 for build and verify, and Haiku 5.5 for searches. Use a different model family for the reviewer. In Copilot, GPT-6.1 Sol costs the same as Sonnet 5.5 ($2 / $10 per 1M input/output tokens) ([Copilot models-and-pricing](https://github.com/github/docs/blob/main/data/tables/copilot/models-and-pricing.yml)). In unattended runs, pin full model IDs rather than aliases, because aliases "update over time". Set `--fallback-model` too ([model config](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/model-config)). Avoid Copilot's `auto` model, because it makes runs hard to reproduce. Keep **Fable off in Copilot**: Anthropic retains its prompts by default, and the zero-retention exemption ends with 2026 ([Supported models](https://docs.github.com/en/copilot/reference/ai-models/supported-models)).

**Logging, tracing and cost.** Both runtimes export OpenTelemetry. Both also accept an inbound `TRACEPARENT`, so the orchestrator can own a root span per ticket and have each agent's spans nest under it. Content capture is **off by default** in both. Keep it off, and store full transcripts in a controlled place instead ([Claude Code monitoring](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/monitoring-usage); [Copilot CLI reference](https://docs.github.com/en/copilot/reference/copilot-cli-reference/cli-command-reference)). Use hard caps: `--max-budget-usd` and `--max-turns` on Claude, and `--max-ai-credits` on Copilot. Both can overshoot by one response, so leave headroom and add a wall-clock timeout of your own ([CLI reference](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/cli-reference)). Good starting caps are about $2 for a plan and $8 for a build, $20 per ticket, and at most 3 fix cycles before escalating. Add a circuit breaker for loops that make no progress. frankbria's Ralph opens it after 3 no-progress loops or 5 same-error loops ([frankbria/ralph-claude-code](https://github.com/frankbria/ralph-claude-code); wiki: `wiki/sources/frankbria-ralph-claude-code.md`). As a reference point, Anthropic's long harness runs cost **$124–$200 per app** ([Anthropic](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/harness-design-long-running-apps)).

**Security.** Never run `--dangerously-skip-permissions` or `--yolo` on your laptop. Use Claude's `dontAsk` mode with an explicit `--allowedTools` list (the docs say it is for "locked-down CI and scripts"). Turn on the OS sandbox with `allowUnsandboxedCommands: false` and `failIfUnavailable: true` ([permission modes](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/permission-modes); [sandboxing](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/sandboxing)). On Copilot, use explicit `--allow-tool`/`--deny-tool` patterns, where deny always wins. Treat ticket text as **untrusted input** and pass it as data. The OpenAI cookbook's Jira workflow pastes the description straight into shell, which is a script-injection risk ([OpenAI cookbook](https://developers.openai.com/cookbook/examples/codex/jira-github)). Break Willison's "lethal trifecta" (private data + untrusted content + a way out) by cutting egress to an allowlist ([Simon Willison](https://simonwillison.net/2025/Oct/22/living-dangerously-with-claude/)). Your planned hooks belong here: protected paths, no test edits during a fix, and a prod-deploy gate. Hooks also hold where prompts don't. Cole's security hook "still fires under `--dangerously-skip-permissions`" ([harness-engineering-demo](https://github.com/coleam00/harness-engineering-demo); wiki: `wiki/sources/coleam00-harness-engineering-demo.md`).

**Quality gates.** Run the gates in this order: deterministic CI first, then a fresh-context reviewer on another model, then a human approves every PR. The reviewer needs the full diff, the ability to run tests, and the intent. One build log found a reviewer that approved a diff after seeing only 6% of it. Later, add a golden set of 20–50 past tickets and run each skill or model change 3 times. Score it with **pass^k, not pass@k**: at a 75% success rate, passing all 3 runs happens only 42% of the time ([Anthropic evals](https://anthropic.com/engineering/demystifying-evals-for-ai-agents)). Then add holdout scenarios that the building agent cannot see.

## The Copilot-only licence decides the runtime, not the design

Your skills use Claude Code features: the Agent tool's per-call `model`, `SendMessage` to send a gap back to the same subagent, `/code-review`, `allowed-tools` with `${CLAUDE_SKILL_DIR}`, and `/add-dir`. At work the agent is Copilot CLI ([our-sdlc](file:///Users/nenaddjordjevic/ai-training/our-sdlc.md)). Several Copilot behaviours your flow depends on are unverified. Your own notes record that `/fleet` subagents can't message each other, that autopilot stops "when it thinks it's done" or after 5 steps, and that `copilot -p` reportedly loses plugin skills on 1.0.92 ([part1-tab2-build](file:///Users/nenaddjordjevic/ai-training/part1-tab2-build.md)). GitHub also recommends Agentic Workflows over `copilot -p` inside CI ([part1-tab1-getting-started](file:///Users/nenaddjordjevic/ai-training/part1-tab1-getting-started.md)).

The answer is the shape Symphony and Warp already use: a **runtime-neutral orchestrator with a runner adapter**. Warp's multi-harness example runs a Claude Code implementer and a Codex reviewer under one foreman ([warp-factory-examples](https://github.com/warpdotdev/warp-factory-examples); wiki: `wiki/sources/warpdotdev-warp-factory-examples.md`). The poller, SQLite, dashboard, locks, caps and Jira write-back never call a model. Only the runner does, either as `claude -p … --output-format stream-json` or as `copilot -p … --output-format=json --share=PATH`. Both runtimes support pinned models, effort, resume, JSON output and OTel, so the adapter stays thin. Prove the Claude path on non-work repos where Claude is allowed. Then prove the Copilot port of `sdlc-ticket` by hand before you automate it at work. The vendor shortcut, Copilot coding agent for Jira, went GA in June 2026 with custom agents, and its Agent Merge "Watch and fix" covers CI failures ([GitHub changelog](https://github.blog/changelog/2026-06-24-github-copilot-for-jira-is-now-generally-available/); [coding agent docs](https://docs.github.com/en/copilot/concepts/agents/coding-agent/about-coding-agent)). It needs a site admin and Rovo, its Actions sessions stop at **59 minutes**, and it runs GitHub's loop instead of your gates. Treat it as a later option for small, well-specified tickets, not as the factory.

## Open decisions you must make

| Decision | Options | Lean |
|---|---|---|
| Runtime at work | Copilot CLI `-p`, GitHub Agentic Workflows, Copilot coding agent | Copilot CLI first (closest to your skills), after phase 0 proves it |
| Where the factory runs | Your Mac, a team VM, GitHub Actions | Mac in phase 1, container or Actions in phase 3 |
| Jira identity | Your personal API token or a service account | Ask security. A service account also stops the factory from reacting to your own edits |
| Trigger signal | Assigned to me / + label / a status | Assigned + `agent-ready` label |
| What the factory may write to Jira on its own | Labels only / plus status transitions / plus comments | Labels only. Comments go through the inbox, per your "approve the text" rule |
| Plan gate | Always / only "big" tickets / never | Always, until data shows otherwise |
| Content in traces | Off / commands only / full | Commands only (`OTEL_LOG_TOOL_DETAILS`), transcripts in a controlled store, retention set by compliance |
| Reviewer model | Same family / other family | Other family (GPT-6.1 Sol in Copilot) |
| Budget | Per stage, per ticket, per month | Start at $20 per ticket and tune from measured data |
| Rovo / Copilot for Jira | Ask the Jira admin or not | Ask now, since it takes a while, but don't depend on it |
| Auto-merge, ever | Never / low-risk repos only | Never without a compliance decision (it's an open question in our-sdlc) |

## Conclusion

The main lesson from the field is that a factory is mostly verification and plumbing, and only a little generation. StrongDM, Cole Medin and Anthropic each succeeded because "done" was decided outside the agent: by holdouts, independent evaluators or human states in the tracker. None of them succeeded through a cleverer prompt. Your SDLC already puts "done" outside the agent. The checks file, the review step, your approval of every post and coworker approval all do that. So the factory's job is narrow: start the run, record it, park it at a decision, and resume it. That also makes the Copilot constraint less painful than it looks. Only the runner adapter depends on the runtime, and the poller, state, dashboard and gates carry over unchanged.

The evidence has gaps. No source publishes measured success, intervention or defect rates for any factory. Several Copilot behaviours your flow needs are untested, and Australian rules (APRA CPS 234/230, data residency) were not researched. Treat phase 1 as an experiment that produces your own numbers. Then let those numbers, not the "lights-out" vision, decide how many gates come out in later phases.
